Reflection

Campus Customs had three tickets on the board on August 31: a student who wanted one white tee in size S, a landlord reminding us rent was due, and the Yale AI Club asking for a deal on 20 hoodies. On paper that's an easy afternoon. I gave it to five agents on gpt-6-luna, 52 model calls and about 117,000 tokens across the three runs, and all three tickets came back stamped resolved. My honest take is that the team was great at finding facts and only okay at thinking across them. When the right answer needed one brain that held all three tickets at once, five brains in separate rooms missed it. The pay tool's guardrail did the thinking for them. Everything below points back to the ticket tabs and the Cash tab on this page.

1. How I'd grade the agents on each ticket

There are three things I care about: did they get the facts right, did they make the right call, and how much did it cost to get there.

Ticket 101, the tee: A−

The facts were all right: 0 tees in S, Bulldog's 5-day lead time, invoice 501 for $840 and 3 days overdue, an $8 purchase order arriving 2026-09-05 at the earliest. They also got the order of operations right without me telling them: pay 501 first, then the tee, because Bulldog won't ship while it's owed money. I signed both, and checking went from $3,400 to $2,552 (Cash tab, rows 1–2). The draft to Tauhid was actually good. It says size S is out, gives the earliest possible date, and promises nothing. What keeps it from an A is cost. It took 16 model calls and 34,259 tokens to sell one $28 tee, and Customer Service ran twice because the Boss asked it for a draft before it had any facts. Don't get me wrong, it worked. It just worked the expensive way.

Ticket 102, the rent: B

The answer was right, and honestly it was the smartest answer of the day: rent is $2,400 due 2026-09-02, it isn't overdue, pay the overdue invoice 501 first, and after both you have $160. That's the one place the agents actually reasoned across tickets. The process was messy though. Facilities and Accounting asked each other the same question in the same second (Facilities → Accounting and Accounting → Facilities at 15:42:40 on the 102 tab), so both of them redid the rent lookup and the invoice lookup. My loop guard only stops a hand-off back to someone already in the same chain. These were two separate chains, so it let them talk past each other. 17 model calls for a rent check. And one Accounting report told the Boss "no payment was prepared" while the other Accounting run had prepared two. Just a nitpick, but in a real business that's the kind of line that gets someone to pay rent twice.

Ticket 103, the hoodies: C

This was the only ticket that needed judgment, and that's where it slipped. The facts were right again: 8 of 20 on hand, short 12, $22 cost, $58 list, $36 margin, $264 to restock 12, Bulldog blocked by 501. But three things went wrong:

  • They missed the cash squeeze. Accounting worked out "$3,400 − $840 − $264 = $2,296 left" and forgot the $2,400 rent from ticket 102. The real number after the bills was $152, so the restock was never affordable. Nobody on the team saw that. The pay tool saw it when I tried to sign (Cash tab, row 5).
  • No discount decision. The ticket is literally a discount request, and the Boss didn't put a number up for approval. Somebody still has to do the actual job.
  • The draft leaked our cost. Customer Service told the club "$22 each ($36 off list) is the maximum price we could offer without selling below cost." That's our floor, sent straight to the customer we're negotiating with. Its prompt says never share cost or margin, but I gave it get_price_and_margin, and it used it.

Add the duplicate work (Inventory ran twice because both the Boss and Accounting asked it) and the duplicate bill (103 prepared invoice 501 again, so the board showed the same $840 twice), and this is the ticket where a person still has all the real work left.

The pattern is obvious once you line them up. My plans were sequential: Boss asks one agent, that agent pulls in exactly one other, done. The real Boss fanned out every time. It sent two or three agents at the start of every ticket, then the specialists also did their own hand-offs, so on 102 and 103 we got the same work done twice. To put it plainly, I planned a relay race and the agents ran a group project. The plan was right about who should sit out on all three tickets, though. Facilities never touched a product ticket, and Inventory never touched rent. So at least the team wasn't sending every agent to every ticket.

One more difference: my plan said the tickets would end at waiting_on_human. In the real board, a ticket is marked resolved the moment the run finishes, and anything left over waits in "Needs your signature." That's why 103 shows resolved even though nothing on it got done.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools

Rent is the easiest call. Ticket 102 is four tool calls in a row: get_rent_status, list_invoices, get_cash_balances, prepare_payment. One agent with those four tools would finish in one loop. The team used 17 model calls, 36,273 tokens and two specialists interviewing each other. Splitting it up didn't add any knowledge, only hand-offs.

101 is the same story. It's a straight line: check stock, find the vendor, notice the invoice, price the payment, write the note. Every step needs what came before it, so splitting it across agents just means writing the facts down and handing them over. You can see that cost in the second Customer Service run that only existed to redo the draft with facts it didn't have the first time.

And the biggest one, which really belongs to all three tickets, is cash planning. The 103 mistake happened because of the team setup. Accounting on 103 ran in its own fresh context, with no idea that Facilities on 102 had just set aside $2,400 for rent. One agent working the whole board, or one Accounting agent that remembers across tickets, would have caught the $152 problem before I ever got to the sign-off tray.

Where the team actually earns its keep is separation of duties: the agent that writes to customers shouldn't be the one that can see cost, and nobody that can talk to customers should be able to move money. But 103 shows that only works if the tools enforce it. A sentence in a prompt isn't a wall. Customer Service should never have had get_price_and_margin.

4. Three new problems this team could solve with the tools I built

1. A club wants 10 Yale caps for an event

Same shape as 103, just easier. Inventory runs check_stock("CC-HAT-BLUE", "OS", 10) and finds 40 on hand, so no restock and no vendor problem. Accounting runs get_price_and_margin: $6 cost, $22 list, $16 margin, so even a generous bulk discount stays above cost. Customer Service drafts the reply, and the Boss puts one discount up for approval. No cash moves, and the hoodie squeeze doesn't matter, because the caps are already on the shelf.

2. Restock the Crest Mug before someone asks for one

The mug is at 0 (check_stock on CC-MUG-CREST). list_vendors shows Elm City Gifts handles small goods, has a 3-day lead time and no unpaid invoices, so it can ship today, unlike Bulldog in all three of our tickets. prepare_purchase_order prices it at $3.50 a mug, so 40 mugs is $140. That actually fits in our $152. A person signs, purchase_with_approval records it, and checking drops to $12. This one's worth doing because it's the only restock the shop can actually afford right now.

3. "Can we make October's rent?"

After we paid on 102, lease 1's next_due moved to 2026-10-02. Facilities can run get_rent_status(1) and Accounting can run get_cash_balances, and together they'd report $2,400 due with $152 in checking: can_cover false. The team can't fix that (see below), but it can raise it a month early, which is the whole point of a back office.

5. Three new problems it couldn't solve, and what it would need

1. Getting paid

Every dollar in this run went out. The Cash tab is three withdrawals and zero deposits, because the data has no revenue and our tools only spend. The Yale AI Club order is 20 hoodies at up to $58 each, real money, and the team has no way to quote it, take a deposit, or record it when it comes in. That's exactly what we'd need to afford the $264 restock that got refused. Would need: an orders table and a customer_invoices table, plus tools like create_quote, create_customer_invoice and record_payment_received (that last one human-confirmed). Plus a Sales / Billing agent that turns an approved discount into a quote and tracks who owes us.

2. Planning cash across the whole board

This is the 103 failure. No tool answers "what do we owe over the next 30 days, and what's left after all of it?" Each agent did math on the one ticket in front of it. Would need: a forecast_cash(days) tool that reads leases, open invoices and every pending approval (the $840, the $8, the $2,400 and the $264 together), and a list_pending_approvals tool so agents can see what's already waiting to be signed. Plus a Treasury agent that the Boss has to check with before anything with a price goes into approvals_needed. It would also stop duplicates, like 103 preparing invoice 501 a second time.

3. Actually closing the loop with customers and stock

Tauhid's tee is "arriving 2026-09-05," but the shop date never moves past August 31, nothing ever receives the order, and inventory for tee S stays at 0 forever. And no draft ever leaves the board, which turned out to be a good thing: the 103 draft would have handed the Yale AI Club our cost. Would need: stock tools like receive_purchase_order (adds to inventory when it arrives) and reserve_stock (holds the 8 hoodies for the club). A send_message tool that only works after a person approves the exact text. A check that blocks any outgoing draft containing cost or margin numbers. And a Fulfillment agent plus a separate Comms reviewer agent whose only job is reading drafts against the shop rules before a person sees them.

So what did I learn?

When you strip away the floor animation, the stamps and the 52 model calls, you're left with a pretty simple lesson. Agents are reliable at looking things up and unreliable at noticing what isn't on their own ticket. Every fact on all three tabs was right. The one thing that would have changed a decision, $152 instead of $2,296, fell between two agents who never talked to each other. The rules that held up were the ones baked into the tools (no agent can pay, no balance below zero, a named human signature), not the ones I wrote into prompts.
